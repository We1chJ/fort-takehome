At the Olin PLai Lab, I spent two years leading the development of an assistive iOS and augmented reality platform designed to help seniors who are chair-bound or bed-bound due to diseases like cancer to exercise remotely at home. The system captured user movement metrics and video feeds from AR glasses and an iOS device, processing posture data through computer vision models to deliver dynamic, low-latency audio guidance and gamified visuals via an integrated AI voice agent.

As one of two founding team members in the lab, I worked on the initial vision of helping elderly patients rehabilitate and exercise conveniently. From brainstorming solutions to architecting the lab’s first prototype, I have been a major contributor to both the design and the implementation. Soon, we brought our first prototype to the users in person, which was an AR glasses tethered directly to an iOS device via wires. This prototype was cost-effective and functional. It performed all intended functions correctly, and initial feedback from our first three users was positive. But after collecting 200+ people’s feedback by visiting the senior centers 4 times a week, I noticed issues from the data we collected from qualitative interviews and observational video logs. Specifically, numerical data from Emotions Survey and Self Consciousness Scale, which quantifies participant's emotions and experience when interacting with the device, suggested a massive user experience bottleneck where over 90% of users reported feeling constrained, anxious, or uncomfortably tied to a machine, and physical wires frequently snagged on hearing aids or traditional eyewear.

Having been sitting down with our target users for more than 100 hours total, I realized that the technology software itself isn’t what they care about at the end. The comfort level and experience of wearing a physical device on their head is what drives them away. To solve this, I researched alternatives that value user experience, and ultimately pivoted to a wireless AR glass setup paired with a dedicated neckband compute unit. This change introduced many technical trade-offs. I had to have higher unit costs, potential battery thermal issues, and the need to optimize private mobile hotspot connectivity to eliminate network latency. The complexity of device setup for users also had to increase, but I decided to prioritize user’s physical comfort first before optimizing user’s mental complexity. I decided to let the data from real users speak for themselves. The subsequent A/B testing with another 20 participants confirmed that eliminating physical tethers increased user immersion by 45%.

This was the biggest pivot throughout the development journey. To support this new architecture, I experienced video mirroring latency issue that didn’t exist before with the wired solution. So I re-engineered the networking layer to prioritize low latency over standard public Wi-Fi by establishing a dedicated local connection between the phone, neckband, and glasses. This allowed our vision model and AI feedback loop to run continuously without lag, securing the nearly same performance as before in terms of real-time posture corrections before a user could overexert themselves. After 18 months of continuous, data-driven iteration, we delivered the latest MVP with 95% user satisfaction rate. 

This experience had been meaningful and memorable for me because it gave me direct exposure to what user-oriented engineering design feels like. When there are endless options and possibilities, the data from the users can be the guide light for the path forward. Every piece of data drives iteration. Every iteration requires balancing factors involving cost, complexity, and user experience.
